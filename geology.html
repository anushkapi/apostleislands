Geologic Setting and History

Apostle Islands National Lakeshore lies within the Superior Upland, marking the southern edge of the Canadian Shield stretching across Wisconsin, Michigan, and Minnesota. This park exposes an ancient geologic history which remains hidden under younger rock throughout much of the country and the Upper Midwest region. Come along and see for yourself!

The Midcontinent Rift and a Billion-Year-Old Sandstone

About 1.1 billion years ago, the North American continent nearly split apart along a feature geologists call the Midcontinent Rift System: a long crack in the crust that stretches from Kansas up through Lake Superior and back down through Michigan. Massive amounts of lava (basaltic) poured out along the rift and this volcanic rock underlies the entire park. Though it never reaches the surface within Apostle Islands, it is buried beneath everything else described on our park tour.

The rift basin then "sagged" and stopped erupting. Rivers and streams began filling it with sand and gravel washed in from surrounding areas. Over tens of millions of years, this sand and gravel hardened into sandstone which now forms every island in the park. Geologists group these rocks into the Bayfield Group, which splits into three separate formations. Each formation tells a unique story. The Orienta Sandstone is the oldest of the three, which was deposited across a broad sand plain. The Devils Island Sandstone is in the middle, formed on sand flats that flooded under shallow water. This intermittent flooding left the rock thinner and softer than the sandstone surrounding it above and below, which turns out to matter a great deal for the shape of the coastline today (see the Rock Features page). The Chequamegon Sandstone is the youngest and highest formation, formed by braided streams similarly to the Orienta.

All in all, this sandstone accumulated from roughly 1 billion to about 660 million years ago, long before any complex life on land existed, hence why it left no fossil record.

Billion-Year+ Gap

Directly above the sandstone sits an unconformity, a surface representing a long stretch of time where no rock was preserved. Geologists have yet to reach a consensus about this; either nothing was deposited, or whatever did accumulate was later stripped away by erosion. Here, that gap spans about 1.1 billion years. Nothing at Apostle Islands records the time span between the end of Bayfield Group deposit and the arrival of Ice Age glaciers, making this a dramatic gap in the rock record unlike anything else in the National Parks.

The Ice Age: Carving the Islands from the Sandstone

More than a billion years after the sandstone hardened, Pleistocene glaciers advanced and retreated across the Lake Superior basin, a process which lasted about 2.6 million years. The last ice cover over this stretch of shoreline retreated only about 12,000 years ago. This moving ice eroded the weaker sandstone that lay between more resistant rock deposits. These deposits, or knobs, survived, were ground down and rounded, and still stand above the surrounding lowland. These rocks eventually became the individual Apostle Islands after the glaciers eroded everything around them.

The glaciers also left material on top of the sandstone. A blanket of till still covers most of the islands and much of the surrounding peninsula today, grouped by geologists into the Miller Creek and Copper Falls Formations. As the ice retreated, melted water from the glaciers pooled between the glacier's edge and higher ground to the south, forming a large body of water known as Glacial Lake Duluth. At its highest stages, this lake submerged nearly all of the Bayfield Peninsula and the islands. Once the ice withdrew completely, the crust slowly rebounded after losing the weight of the ice sheet; this process is called isostatic rebound. New drainage pathways opened, and lake levels eventually dropped slowly. The islands and peninsula reemerged during this process, and became the landscape park visitors behold today.

A Place of Geologic Significance

Apostle Islands National Lakeshore contains two officially designated geologic type sections. These reference exposures that geologists use to define a rock unit for the greater region. Having two reference points within a single park unit is uncommon and shows how directly the Apostle Islands' cliffs and shorelines expose rock that is buried across most of the Upper Midwest.
