Geologic Setting and History

Apostle Islands National Lakeshore lies within the Superior Upland, marking the southern edge of the Canadian Shield stretching across Wisconsin, Michigan, and Minnesota. This park exposes an ancient geologic history which remains hidden under younger rock throughout much of the country. Come along and see for yourself!

The Midcontinent Rift and a Billion-Year-Old Sandstone

About 1.1 billion years ago, the North American continent nearly split apart along a feature geologists call the Midcontinent Rift System, a long crack in the crust that runs from present-day Kansas up through Lake Superior and back down through Michigan. Enormous volumes of basaltic lava poured out along the rift; this volcanic rock underlies the entire park, though it never reaches the surface within Apostle Islands, sitting instead as buried basement rock beneath everything else described on this page.

The rift basin then sagged and stopped erupting, and rivers and streams began filling it with sand and gravel washed in from surrounding highlands. Over tens of millions of years, this sediment hardened into the sandstone that now forms every island in the park. Geologists group these rocks into the Bayfield Group, which splits into three separate formations, each with its own story. The Orienta Sandstone is the oldest and lowest of the three; northeastward-flowing braided streams deposited it across a broad sand plain. The Devils Island Sandstone is sandwiched in the middle, formed on sand flats that flooded intermittently under shallow, ponded water; that stop-and-start deposition left it thinner-bedded and softer than the sandstone above and below it, which turns out to matter a great deal for the shape of the coastline today (see the Rock Features page). The Chequamegon Sandstone is the youngest and uppermost formation, laid down by braided streams much like the Orienta.

Altogether, this sandstone accumulated from roughly 1 billion to about 660 million years ago, long before any complex life on land existed; that is why it left no fossil record.

A Gap of More Than a Billion Years

Directly above the sandstone sits an unconformity, a surface representing a long stretch of time with no rock preserved; either nothing was deposited, or whatever did accumulate was later stripped away by erosion. Here, that gap spans roughly 1.1 billion years. Nothing at Apostle Islands records the span between the end of Bayfield Group deposition and the arrival of Ice Age glaciers, making this one of the more dramatic gaps in the rock record anywhere in the National Park System.

The Ice Age: Carving the Islands from the Sandstone

More than a billion years after the sandstone hardened, Pleistocene glaciers began advancing and retreating across the Lake Superior basin, doing so repeatedly over roughly the last 2.6 million years. The last ice lobe to cover this stretch of shoreline pulled back only about 12,000 years ago, a brief moment against the age of the rock underneath it. Moving ice eroded the fractured, weaker sandstone that lay between more resistant rock knobs; those knobs survived, ground down and rounded, still standing above the surrounding lowland, and they became the individual Apostle Islands. In effect, the islands are not something the glaciers built; they are what the glaciers left behind after eroding everything softer around them.

The glaciers also left material on top of the sandstone. A blanket of till still covers most of the islands and much of the surrounding peninsula today, grouped by geologists into the Miller Creek and Copper Falls Formations. As the ice retreated, meltwater pooled between the glacier's edge and higher ground to the south, forming a large body of water known as Glacial Lake Duluth; at its highest stages, this lake submerged nearly all of the Bayfield Peninsula and the islands. Once the ice fully withdrew, the crust slowly rebounded after losing the weight of the ice sheet, a process called isostatic rebound. New drainage outlets opened, and lake levels dropped in stages; the islands and peninsula gradually reemerged, arriving at roughly the configuration visitors see today.

A Place of Geologic Significance

Apostle Islands National Lakeshore contains two officially designated geologic type sections, reference exposures that geologists use to formally define a rock unit for the wider region. Having two such reference points within a single park unit is uncommon; it shows how directly the Apostle Islands' cliffs and shorelines expose rock that stays buried across most of the Upper Midwest.
