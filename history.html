Human History

Indigenous Roots

The Apostle Islands and the Bayfield Peninsula are ancestral homeland of the Ojibwe people; they knew Madeline Island as Mooningwanekaaning and Lake Superior as Gitchigumi, both of which are and were of great spiritual significance to them. Throughout the area, archaeological sites have revealed human settlements going back to the Archaic period. Long before the European settlers arrived, unpredictable, violent storms and rocky shoals made the surrounding waters precarious.

Fur Trade; First European Settlements

In the 1600s, European settlers including French fur traders arrived in the region. These settlers found an established Ojibwe presence and the Europeans adopted the name "Chequamegon" for the surrounding bay, which comes from an Ojibwe word describing a shallow, sandy inlet. La Pointe, on Madeline Island, grew into center for the regional fur trade. French traders ran the operation at first, and later the American Fur Company took over. Beaver pelts were its longest-running commodity. However, nineteenth-century treaties eventually gave much of the surrounding land to the US government. Subsequently, Ojibwe communities faced pressure to relocate to newly created reservations. Despite this pressure, many families kept ties to the islands, who retained hunting and fishing rights that are still legally recognized today.

Industrialization Post-1800

In the 1800s, the fur trade began declining. Commercial fishing, logging of old-growth pine and hemlock, and brownstone quarrying took its place as the island economy's main activities. Quarries on Basswood, Hermit, and Stockton Islands cut sandstone. Workers shipped it to Chicago, Milwaukee, and other Midwestern cities for use in storefronts and houses. The Basswood Island quarry opened in 1868. It grew into a pit some 400 feet by 300 feet. The nationwide economic panic of 1893 curtailed most quarrying activity in the islands for good.

Lighthouses

The storms on Lake Superior made the waters around the Apostle Islands treacherous for the schooners and iron-ore freighters passing through. Congress began funding lighthouse construction nationwide since the Lighthouse Act of 1789 and so, between 1856 and 1915, a series of light stations were constructed across the archipelago to guide ships safely past its rocky shore. Historian F. Ross Holland described the result as the largest and finest single collection of lighthouses in the country. Notable lighthouses include the Old Michigan Island Lighthouse built 1856, which was the first in the islands. Additional lighthouses of significance include the Raspberry Island Lighthouse (1862), the Sand Island Lighthouse (1881), the Devils Island Light Tower (1898), and the Ashland Harbor Breakwater Lighthouse (1915).

The Park Today

On September 26, 1970, congress officially established Apostle Islands National Lakeshore, championed by Wisconsin democratic senator Gaylord Nelson. This designation helps protect the twenty-one islands and mainland shoreline in the park. While commercial fishing continues today, it is on a far smaller and much more tightly regulated scale than in the nineteenth century. Furthermore, Ojibwe communities are presently active partners with the National Park Service, helping manage the islands' cultural and natural resources.
