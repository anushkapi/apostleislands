Environmental Issues and Pressures

Apostle Islands National Lakeshore is not a static museum piece; instead, the geologic and human forces that built and carved the islands are still active. Several newer pressures also shape how the National Park Service manages the archipelago; some are environmental, and some are tied to the park's human history.

Ongoing Erosion

Shoreline erosion has continued steadily since the glaciers retreated, described on the Geologic Setting and History page. Storms, swinging lake levels, and loosely consolidated glacial till keep the process active. Much of the coast is covered in this till and in places in the region, erosion is accelerating. Bluff retreat threatens trails, historic structures, and archaeological sites in some locations around the park.

Global Warming on Lake Superior

The lake's winter ice cover has grown noticeably thinner and more sparse in recent decades. This symptom of global warming has far-reaching effects in the Great Lakes region; for example, the park's popular ice caves near Meyers Beach need ice coverage to be above roughly 90 percent on the entire lake to be safely accessible to visitors. The lake's long-term average maximum ice cover, measured since 1973, is closer to 60 percent. As a result, a safe opening of the ice caves has become an increasingly rare event, not a dependable winter attraction. Reduced ice cover also leaves the shoreline exposed to wave energy for a larger share of the year, worsening erosion.

Invasive Species

Invasive zebra and quagga mussels are already present elsewhere in the Great Lakes. These species threaten to reduce water clarity and destroy the food web if they reach the park's waters. Land managers are also tracking invasive plants in the region which are competing with the islands' rare dune, bog, and cliff-edge species, described on the Ecology page. A warming lake and lower water levels in some years make it easier for these species to spread than in the past.

Water Quality and Microplastics

Because the park has no inland lakes of its own, water quality monitoring focuses entirely on Lake Superior itself. In recent years, the islands have become a research site for tracking microplastics in the lake, which is part of a broader effort across the US by park scientists.

Land Disputes

The pressures on the Apostle Islands are not exclusively environmental. As described on the Human History page, while nineteenth-century treaties and European settlement pushed Ojibwe communities toward reservations, their spiritual ties to the islands continued. The economic booms and busts of quarrying, logging, and fishing scarred the landscape, with abandoned quarries and depleted forests, before conservation efforts proliferated. The park's 1970 founding reflects this shift toward a more progressive, ecologically-minded attitude toward nature.
