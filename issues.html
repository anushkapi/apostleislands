Environmental Issues and Pressures

The geologic and human forces we've covered that built the Apostle Islands remain active today, continuously shaping the park. Newer pressures continue to influence how the National Park Service manages the islands, some of which are environmental, some of which are human.

Ongoing Erosion

Shoreline erosion has continued since the glaciers retreated, as described on the Geologic Setting and History page. Storms, swinging lake levels, and loosely consolidated glacial till keep the process active. Much of the coast is covered in this till and in places in the region, erosion is accelerating. Bluff retreat threatens trails, historic structures, and archaeological sites in some locations around the park.

Global Warming on Lake Superior

The lake's winter ice cover has grown noticeably thinner and more sparse in recent decades. This symptom of global warming has far-reaching effects in the Great Lakes region; for example, the park's popular ice caves near Meyers Beach need ice coverage to be above roughly 90 percent on the entire lake to be safely accessible to visitors. The lake's long-term average maximum ice cover, measured since 1973, is closer to 60 percent. Nowadays, the ice caves' safe opening has become increasingly rare, whereas it used to be a dependable winter attraction for tourists in the Midwest. The reduced ice cover also leaves the shoreline exposed to waves for a larger portion of the year which worsens erosion in the park.

Invasive Species

Invasive zebra and quagga mussels are already present elsewhere in the Great Lakes. These species threaten to reduce water clarity and destroy the food web if they reach the park's waters. Land managers are also tracking invasive plants in the region which are competing with the islands' rare dune, bog, and cliff-edge species, described on the Ecology page. A warming lake and lower water levels in some years make it easier for these species to spread than in the past.

Water Quality and Microplastics

Because the park has no inland lakes of its own, water quality monitoring focuses entirely on Lake Superior itself. In recent years, the islands have become a research site for tracking microplastics in the lake, which is part of a broader effort across the US by park scientists.

Land Disputes

The pressures on the Apostle Islands are not limited to environmental factors. As described on the Human History page, while nineteenth-century treaties and European settlement pushed Ojibwe communities toward reservations, their spiritual ties to the islands continued even after they were de-facto expelled. The economic variability industry including quarrying, logging, and fishing marred the natural landscape. Visitors can see remnants of these activities in the park's abandoned quarries, for example. The park's 1970 founding reflects a shift toward a more progressive, ecologically-minded attitude toward nature, rejecting the previous idea that nature was merely a resource to be extracted.
