Rock Features and Landforms

The Geologic Setting and History page explains what the islands are made of; this page covers what is happening to that rock right now, as Lake Superior's waves, ice, and currents continue to reshape the shoreline year after year.

The park's best-known, most striking feature is its sea caves, which are carved into the Devils Island Sandstone. Waves "attack" the weakest layers first, which hollows out chambers, arches, and passageways along the cliff base. In the winter, spray freezes into elaborate ice formations inside the caves, drawing visitors to the park at all times of the year.

Sea caves cut into the Devils Island Sandstone along the island's north shore, with the Devils Island Light visible in the distance.
Photo: NPS / G.M. Spoto. Source: https://www.nps.gov/apis/learn/nature/geologicformations.htm

Glacial till bluffs erode and release sand, while longshore currents redistribute this sand along the shore. This builds a family of related landforms called sandscapes. A spit is a narrow ridge of sand extending from the shore into open water, while a tombolo is a sandbar that grows far enough to connect an island to a smaller neighboring island. A cuspate foreland is a wide, blunt point of built-up sand; it is essentially a spit that never got narrow enough to count as one. All three of these landforms show up repeatedly around the park and continue to actively form and shift with each storm season.

A sandspit built out from the southern tip of Outer Island, formed as longshore currents move sand eroded from nearby glacial bluffs.
Photo: NPS / Billy Flynn. Source: https://www.nps.gov/apis/index.htm

Stepped, flat platforms sit above today's shoreline, marking places where Lake Superior's water level once stood higher; the old water level cut a bench into the rock, or left a terrace of sediment, before dropping to a new level. The lake fell through a series of distinct stages after the Ice Age, described on the geology page, so each terrace or bench shows a specific former high-water stand instead of a single, gradual retreat.

Splashpools remind visitors that the flat rock high above the waterline is still subject to the same weathering patterns that carve the sea caves below it.
