Ecology

The Apostle Islands are cut off from the mainland by open water and due to this, they preserve habitats that have become uncommon elsewhere in Wisconsin. Natural water barriers, low development pressure, and steep, rocky terrain have all helped protect these plant and animal communities, which logging, farming, and roads eliminated across much of the rest of the state.

Habitats

Pockets of old-growth forest survive on several islands: hemlock, white pine, and cedar. In some places, this is because historically lighthouse keepers kept the surrounding timber off-limits to logging crews. Elsewhere, thin soil over bare sandstone supports distinct maritime cliff plant communities. Clay bluffs, bog and lagoon wetlands, and dune systems each host their own narrow set of species; the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources has designated Maritime Forest, Sandscape, and Maritime Cliff communities on several islands as official State Natural Areas, formal recognition that little comparable habitat remains anywhere else in the state.

Wildlife

A wide range of animals persists on a small portion of land here. This is due to the fact that few of the 21 park islands have roads or year-round human settlement. Species in the park are not always easy to spot, though they can include bald eagles, herring gulls, common loons, double-crested cormorants, black bears, white-tailed deer, river otters, beavers, and coyotes; herring gulls nest in large colonies on several islands.

A black bear swims between islands in the park. Bears regularly cross open water within the archipelago, which is part of why the National Park Service maintains a dedicated bear safety program for visitors.
