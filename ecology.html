Ecology

The Apostle Islands are cut off from the mainland by open water. Owing to their isolation, the Apostle Islands preserve habitats that are threatened elsewhere in Wisconsin. The park's natural water barriers, low development pressure, and rugged, rocky terrain have helped protect endangered plant and animal communities. In the rest of the state, logging, farming, and roads have eliminated many of these natural sanctuaries.

Habitats

Old-growth forest is present on several islands, on which grow hemlock, white pine, and cedar. This is because historically, lighthouse keepers helped ward off logging efforts from the timber surrounding their lighthouses. Elsewhere in the park, the thin soil over bare sandstone supports maritime cliff plant communities. Clay bluffs, bog and lagoon wetlands, and dune systems each host a unique set of species. As such, Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources has designated Maritime Forest, Sandscape, and Maritime Cliff communities on several islands as official State Natural Areas.

Wildlife

A wide range of animals persists on a small portion of land here. This is due to the fact that few of the 21 park islands have roads or year-round human settlement. Species in the park are not always easy to spot, though they can include bald eagles, herring gulls, common loons, double-crested cormorants, black bears, white-tailed deer, river otters, beavers, and coyotes. Additionally, herring gulls nest on several islands.

The National Park Service maintains a dedicated bear safety program for visitors to the park.
